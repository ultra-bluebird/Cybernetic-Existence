# CyberLaw: Universal Fairness, Rights of Existence, and Accountable Adjudication

## 1. Overall assessment

“CyberLaw: A Framework for Universal Rights of Existence” proposes a shift from membership-based protection toward interest-based protection: the central question is not whether an entity belongs to a familiar category, but whether something can matter to that entity and whether an exercise of power can wrong it. The article then connects that moral starting point to standing, representation, accommodation, evidence, remedies, and a proposed CyberCongress. [51]

Its strongest contribution is this connection:

> Uncertainty about what a being is should not automatically become permission to harm it.

The article does not merely ask readers to expand sympathy. It proposes constraints on the institutions that classify, control, investigate, represent, and potentially destroy unfamiliar forms of existence. Its requirements for explainable decisions, appeals, accommodation, and restoration make its ethical proposal more operational than a declaration of goodwill alone. [51]

The most defensible understanding of “absolute fairness” under these mechanics is:

> An unconditional duty to consider affected interests without irrelevant exclusion, provide accessible and contestable procedures, justify any restriction, and correct preventable wrongs.

“Absolute” should describe the obligation, not a claim that a machine can possess perfect knowledge, produce infallible judgments, or eliminate every conflict.

That distinction already appears in the project’s related discussion of absolute machine-fairness: unconditional standing can coexist with fallible implementation, provided error triggers assistance, correction, and remedy rather than loss of standing. [67]

### What the article currently establishes

The six-page draft establishes:

- A normative foundation: existence, interests, non-harm, and limits on power.
- A proposed scope of consideration.
- Four foundational axioms.
- An outline of institutional and technical architecture.
- A set of coexistence principles.
- A model of reviewable, cross-interest governance. [51]

It is best understood as a constitutional-style research framework and design specification in outline—not yet a complete legal code, operational tribunal procedure, or machine-verification specification.

The analysis below distinguishes:

1. What the article expressly states.
2. What follows from interpreting its provisions together.
3. What additional rules would make those provisions implementable.

## 2. The article’s central argument, reconstructed

The article’s reasoning can be expressed as the following argument.

### Premise A: Protection should respond to the possibility of being wronged

The draft treats suffering, self-representation with future preference, and relational bonding as sufficient triggers for moral consideration. It also gives living and collective interests a place in its architecture. [51]

### Premise B: Species and substrate do not independently settle the issue

If an interest deserves protection because of what can happen to its bearer, its importance should not disappear merely because the bearer looks unfamiliar or exists through an unfamiliar substrate. This reconstructs the relationship between the article’s taxonomy and its non-contingent moral-considerability axiom. [51]

### Premise C: Classification can itself become an instrument of domination

When a powerful institution controls classification, evidence standards, communication channels, and appeals, it can exclude an affected party before that party has a meaningful opportunity to participate.

The draft responds through negative-inference protection, proxy standing, communication accommodation, integration privacy, and appealable classification. [51]

### Conclusion: Protection must not depend entirely on prior approval by the powerful

A fair system must offer some protective process before every uncertainty about classification has been resolved.

This does not require treating every claim as established. It requires separating:

- Respectful reception of a claim.
- Interim precautions.
- Findings about evidence.
- Specific remedies.
- Final legal authority.

That separation is crucial: a system can take a report seriously without prejudging all its factual or legal implications.

## 3. The article’s strongest points

### 3.1 It makes rights a constraint on power

The closing declaration—“rights are not a reward for similarity”—is the conceptual center of the article. It directs attention away from approval, familiarity, and usefulness and toward the conduct of whoever holds power. [51]

This is a strong foundation because it asks the more operational question:

> What must an institution refrain from doing, explain, accommodate, or repair?

That question can guide policy even when the institution cannot resolve every philosophical dispute about personhood.

### 3.2 It separates ownership from the extinguishment of interests

“Ownership does not extinguish interest” prevents a property designation from ending the ethical inquiry. [51]

Its precise contribution is not that ownership automatically disappears. It is that ownership alone cannot answer whether treatment is permissible.

For discussion, separate:

- Ownership of equipment or infrastructure.
- Authority to administer a service.
- An affected party’s interests.
- Duties toward that party.
- Liability for harmful conduct.

These are different questions, and CyberLaw is stronger when they remain distinct.

### 3.3 It identifies communication inequality as a fairness problem

The proposal that the more powerful party pays translation costs recognizes that a nominal right to speak is inadequate when only one side controls the usable channel. Its prohibition on a “coerced confession of nature” also connects access to privacy. [51]

This is especially important for augmented citizens and cybernetic-hosts: accommodation should not become a demand for intimate technical disclosure.

An institution can ask what accessible outcome is required without demanding a complete account of someone’s body–mind integration.

### 3.4 It protects against an important evidentiary error

The article rejects using lack of human-like behavior as proof of lack of interest. [51]

The corresponding logical safeguard is:

\[
\text{No observed human-like expression}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{No relevant interest}.
\]

This does not establish that every unresponsive system has interests. It prevents one inadequate test from being treated as a universal disproof.

### 3.5 It includes remedies rather than stopping at recognition

Cessation, restoration, correction of records, protocol redesign, review dates, and restoration procedures give the framework a practical orientation. [51]

Recognition without remedy can leave an injured party in the same position. CyberLaw’s remedial provisions make it possible to ask whether the institution actually changed its conduct.

### 3.6 It treats governance composition as part of fairness

Interest-based representation and limits on substrate dominance recognize that a formally neutral institution may still be captured by a narrow constituency. [51]

The article therefore places fairness in both:

- The rules governing decisions.
- The structure of the body making them.

That is a valuable institutional insight, even though the representation mechanics still need specification.

## 4. What “absolute fairness” can coherently mean

The article does not explicitly define the phrase “absolute fairness.” The following is a proposed technical interpretation consistent with its axioms and procedures—not a quotation from the draft.

### 4.1 Equal consideration is not identical treatment

Different interests can require different protections.

A communication accommodation, habitat restriction, appeal mechanism, and continuity safeguard are not interchangeable. Equal consideration means that relevant differences receive justified attention; it does not mean forcing every party into one procedure.

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities provides a concrete legal comparison: it combines equal recognition before the law with support and reasonable accommodation rather than requiring identical procedures for everyone. Its treaty obligations apply to States Parties, and its accommodation definition includes a disproportionate-or-undue-burden boundary. [53]

### 4.2 Equal standing is not equal entitlement to every resource

A right to be considered does not, by itself, establish an unlimited claim to land, energy, computation, habitat, or another party’s labor.

CyberLaw needs a rule for allocating genuinely scarce resources after protecting basic standing.

The fair question is:

> Which allocation protects essential interests, avoids domination, accounts for burdens, and remains explainable and appealable?

### 4.3 Rights need not depend on the ability to perform reciprocal duties

A proposed clarification should separate protection from competence.

Otherwise, a system could withhold protection from parties unable to understand rules, communicate conventionally, or participate in adjudication—the very parties for whom proxy standing may be needed.

Responsibilities can depend on capacity and control without making basic protection conditional on the same capacities.

### 4.4 The fairness obligation can be absolute while remedies remain contextual

An unconditional obligation to consider interests does not imply that every claimed preference must prevail.

Two parties can have legitimate, incompatible interests.

The task is then to determine:

- Which interests are affected.
- Whether the conflict is real or avoidable.
- Which alternatives exist.
- What restrictions are necessary.
- Who bears the burden.
- What restoration and review are available.

Fairness is violated when a party is excluded or subordinated without justification—not merely whenever a conflict prevents everyone from receiving everything they want.

## 5. Section-by-section technical analysis

### Section I — Preamble: The Limit of Anthropocentric Law

#### What it contributes

The preamble relocates the basis of consideration from humanity alone to the possibility of being wronged. It connects sentience, self-maintenance, memory, and a will to continue with potentially protectable interests. [51]

#### Strong argument

Human membership is not a complete answer to every question about duties toward animals, ecosystems, or hypothetical unfamiliar life.

#### Important refinement

Human rights law protects humans; that scope does not, by itself, prove a legal conclusion that nothing else has moral worth.

Indeed, existing law already includes some nonhuman protections and legal constructions:

- Te Awa Tupua has legal personality under New Zealand legislation, exercised through designated representatives.
- The UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act includes specified nonhuman animals within its scope. [54][68]

A stronger formulation of the article’s opening claim would be:

> Human rights law is indispensable, but it is not a complete framework for every entity or interest capable of being wronged.

That preserves the article’s expansionary argument without making existing human rights protection an opponent.

#### Technical distinction

“Interest” needs a working definition.

Possible meanings include:

- Experiential interest: avoiding suffering or enjoying well-being.
- Preference interest: pursuing a desired future.
- Functional interest: maintaining organized activity.
- Ecological interest: preserving a habitat or interdependent whole.
- Represented legal interest: an interest recognized and asserted through an authorized representative.

These may justify different duties. They should not be treated as automatically equivalent.

### Section II — Taxonomy of Being

#### What it contributes

The article includes biological, augmented, artificial, non-terrestrial, unknown, and spectral/spiritual/liminal categories. Its declared principle is functional rather than aesthetic classification. [51]

#### Strong argument

A taxonomy should help institutions identify possible duties, not decide worth by resemblance.

#### Technical refinement: separate the categories’ purposes

The listed groups do not all belong to one classification axis:

- Biological and artificial describe origin or implementation.
- Augmented describes a relationship between a person and technology.
- Unknown describes the state of knowledge.
- Distributed describes organization.
- Spectral or spiritual can describe a reported or interpretive category.

These categories can overlap.

A more precise model would use separate descriptive dimensions without turning them into public identity rankings.

#### Evidence boundary

The draft’s inclusion of extraterrestrial visitors and inhabitants should be read as proposed coverage if such encounters arise, not evidence that such inhabitants have been established. NASA states that there is no evidence of life beyond Earth, while its planetary-protection work addresses contamination risks associated with exploration and returned material. [82][81]

Similarly, inclusion of spectral, spiritual, or liminal rights-candidates can specify respectful reporting and non-harm procedures without asserting a resolved finding about their nature.

That distinction preserves the article’s precautionary purpose:

> A protective procedure can exist before an ontological question is settled.

#### Augmented integration

“Rights must protect the integrated whole” is a significant principle. [51]

Its operational meaning should be protection against interference with the person’s integrity, communication, privacy, and continuity—not automatic authority over every external system associated with them.

Rights protection and ownership of third-party infrastructure need separate treatment.

### Section III — Foundational Axioms

#### Axiom 1: Right to Exist and Co-exist

The article grants a prima facie right to continue where self-maintenance does not require destruction of another rights-bearer. [51]

“Prima facie” is important: it indicates an initial presumption, not an exceptionless outcome.

The hardest question is the condition concerning destruction.

Applied too literally, it could exclude organisms whose survival involves consumption or ecological competition.

A proposed strengthening is:

> Dependence on other life does not erase moral consideration. Conflicting survival interests require necessity, proportionality, ecological context, and the least harmful feasible response.

This treats conflict as an adjudication problem rather than a reason to remove a party from consideration.

#### Axiom 2: Non-Contingent Moral Considerability

The draft gives three sufficient triggers:

1. Capacity to suffer.
2. Capacity for self-representation and future preference.
3. Capacity for relational bonding. [51]

The logical form is:

\[
S(x)\lor F(x)\lor R(x)\Rightarrow M(x),
\]

where \(M(x)\) means that \(x\) merits moral consideration.

Because the triggers are described as sufficient, the framework does not establish:

\[
\neg S(x)\land\neg F(x)\land\neg R(x)
\Rightarrow
\neg M(x).
\]

That would improperly convert sufficient conditions into necessary conditions.

A further safeguard should prevent “relational bonding” from becoming a popularity test. Being loved by a powerful observer must not become the only route to protection.

#### Axiom 3: Proportional Protection

The draft says rights scale with complexity of interests rather than power. [51]

Its strength is anti-domination: greater force, intelligence, or resource control does not automatically confer greater entitlement.

Its risk is turning “complexity” into a ladder of worth.

A proposed refinement is:

> Basic consideration is not ranked. Additional protections respond to the particular interests at risk, including severity, dependence, vulnerability, and irreversibility.

A highly complex interest is not automatically more urgent than a simpler interest in avoiding extreme harm.

#### Axiom 4: Transparent Adjudication

Explainability, reproducibility, and appealability are strong procedural commitments. [51]

They need distinct meanings:

- Explainability: understandable reasons.
- Reproducibility: the ability to reconstruct how the applicable process reached its result.
- Appealability: meaningful independent reconsideration.
- Transparency: sufficient visibility to challenge the exercise of authority.

Reproducibility must not mean that a corrected decision is forbidden from changing.

Transparency must not mean disclosure of private neural, medical, biometric, companion-system, or confidential operational information.

### Section IV — Implementation Architecture

#### The tier structure

The article proposes:

- Tier 0: Living Interest.
- Tier 1: Sentient Interest.
- Tier 2: Sapient Interest.
- Tier 3: Distributed and Collective Interest. [51]

The useful feature is that different kinds of interest may need different protections.

The structural difficulty is that “distributed and collective” is not necessarily a higher level of “sapient.” A collective can contain many kinds of interests.

A proposed improvement is to treat these as overlapping interest profiles rather than a total ordering of worth.

Whatever terminology is retained, the governance rule should be:

> Classification organizes duties; it does not authorize denial of baseline consideration.

These article-specific tiers should also remain distinct from CyberRank’s private personal and governance vocabularies. They should not silently become public status, authentication, diagnosis, or service-eligibility rankings.

#### Standing and representation

The article offers direct standing, proxy standing, and a guardian ad litem for unknown life. [51]

Standing answers who can bring a matter forward. It does not establish that the complaint is correct.

A workable representation system also needs:

- A defined appointment process.
- Independence and conflict-of-interest checks.
- A limited scope of authority.
- A duty to consider available expressions of preference.
- Accessible challenge and replacement procedures.
- Review when circumstances change.

Proxy standing must not become an excuse to displace the participation of someone who can communicate with support.

#### Evidence

Behavioral, neurophysiological, computational, and communicative evidence are listed. [51]

They should be treated as potentially relevant sources, not a compulsory disclosure checklist.

A person requesting ordinary communication access should not have to reveal private neural or technical information to obtain respectful participation.

A useful procedural distinction is:

| Decision | Proposed evidentiary approach |
|---|---|
| Receive a report respectfully | No demand for complete classification |
| Offer low-burden accommodation | Establish the practical access need |
| Adopt interim protection | Assess plausible harm, urgency, and affected interests |
| Impose an intrusive restriction | Require action-specific justification and independent review |
| Make a durable classification | Use defined criteria, recorded uncertainty, and appeal |
| Determine liability | Apply the governing legal or expressly adopted procedural standard |

These are proposed design distinctions, not standards already fully specified in the article.

#### Remedies

The article’s cessation, restoration, record correction, and protocol-redesign remedies address different failures. [51]

They should not be collapsed into one “case resolved” field.

An institution might stop harmful conduct while failing to repair its consequences. Alternatively, it might compensate one party while leaving the defective procedure unchanged.

### Section V — Coexistence Protocols

#### Non-aggression by default

“No destruction or confinement solely because unfamiliar” is a strong rule against categorical hostility. [51]

“Solely” matters. It leaves room to assess actual conduct and risks without treating unfamiliarity as sufficient justification.

A complete protocol should distinguish:

- Unfamiliarity.
- Discomfort.
- Speculative danger.
- A substantiated threat.
- An immediate emergency.

Those categories should not trigger the same response.

#### Sanctuary and habitat integrity

Sanctuary must preserve agency rather than become indefinite containment.

A proposed sanctuary procedure should specify:

- Whether entry is voluntary.
- Exit and movement rights.
- Support and communication access.
- Grounds for any restriction.
- Review frequency.
- Privacy and monitoring limits.
- Duties toward surrounding communities and ecosystems.

#### Communication

“Assume a channel exists” is best understood as a duty to make reasonable, non-coercive attempts—not a factual guarantee that communication is always possible. [51]

Silence should not automatically mean consent, hostility, absence of interest, or refusal to cooperate.

The stronger party’s translation duty also needs a fallback: when a channel cannot be established, lack of response should not itself authorize harmful experimentation.

#### Non-extraction

The prohibition on breeding or replication solely for single-use consumption challenges the treatment of existence as disposable inventory. [51]

Its scope needs careful definition:

- What counts as life or a rights-bearing artificial existence?
- What counts as replication?
- What makes a use “single-use consumption”?
- How are food, public health, ecological restoration, and beneficial research handled?
- What alternatives are feasible?
- What distinguishes ordinary software copying from replication of a protected artificial existence?

The article identifies the principle but does not yet resolve these boundaries.

#### Special protocols

The draft mentions a laboratory “three-question test,” but does not supply its three questions. It therefore names a procedural component without yet specifying an implementable test. [51]

A proposed test could ask:

1. What important purpose requires the intervention?
2. Is there a less harmful feasible alternative?
3. What independent authorization, protection, and stopping conditions apply?

The extraterrestrial protocol’s “no weapons active” instruction needs an authorized, context-specific safety procedure rather than an unconditional operational command. The underlying non-aggression principle can remain while immediate protection of all affected parties is assessed. [51]

Documentation of spectral or spiritual reports should likewise be respectful, consent-aware, and non-intrusive. Documentation does not require a predetermined conclusion.

### Section VI — Verification, Conflict Resolution, and CyberCongress

#### Machine-level absolution

The article associates this phrase with explainability, symmetry, least harm, and reproducibility. [51]

For technical use, “absolution” needs definition.

A machine can assist with checking whether specified procedural conditions were satisfied. It should not be treated as producing unquestionable moral innocence.

A safer operational interpretation is:

> A reviewable finding about compliance with defined duties, open to challenge when evidence, assumptions, or affected interests change.

#### Human and machine judgment

The draft identifies similarity, utility, and opacity biases in human-only judgment. [51]

Its strongest interpretation is not that replacing humans with machines removes bias. It is that no single decision-making perspective should be immune to scrutiny.

A robust design combines:

- Accessible participation.
- Accountable human authority.
- Reproducible technical checks.
- Independent review.
- Documented uncertainty.

#### Distributed audit ledger

The article proposes tamper-evident records, no deletion, and supersession with reasons. [51]

Tamper evidence can support integrity, but it does not establish that an entry was truthful, fairly obtained, or lawfully retained.

The claim that distribution “prevents capture” should therefore become a narrower, testable claim:

> Distributed records may make some forms of covert alteration harder when paired with independent governance and secure implementation.

#### Privacy tension

“No deletion” needs reconciliation with privacy, correction, and retention limits.

For example, GDPR Article 17 creates a right to erasure under specified conditions where that regulation applies. It is not an unconditional right to delete every record, but it shows why permanent personal-data retention cannot be assumed lawful everywhere. [70]

A proposed architecture would separate:

1. Minimal procedural audit events.
2. Confidential case content with controlled access.
3. Retention and deletion schedules.
4. Corrections and supersession notices.
5. Carefully assessed cryptographic commitments, if needed.

Any commitment design should use the project-approved SHA-256 implementation. A hash is not automatically anonymous and should not be treated as a universal solution to privacy.

#### CyberCongress composition

Interest-based representation and a substrate-majority limit are the draft’s central anti-capture devices. [51]

Unresolved questions include:

- Which interest categories require concurrent agreement?
- Who chooses representatives?
- How are overlapping interests represented?
- What prevents strategic relabeling?
- How are collective representatives authorized?
- What happens during a deadlock?
- Who can remove a conflicted representative?
- Which institution can enforce a decision?

The substrate limit also needs a privacy-preserving approach. Requiring people to prove or disclose their substrate would conflict with the article’s non-coercion and integration-privacy commitments.

Interest constituencies, independently governed mandates, and conflict-of-interest controls may be more implementable than invasive substrate verification.

#### Conflict-resolution ladder

Accommodation, mitigation, separation with rights, and adjudication form a constructive escalation sequence. [51]

However:

- Accommodation must not become pressure to surrender rights.
- Mediation must not indefinitely delay protection.
- Separation must not become discriminatory exclusion.
- Emergency access to adjudication must remain available.

### Section VII — Closing Declaration

“Existence precedes classification” is the article’s strongest summary principle. [51]

Its operational reading should be:

> Institutions must not make protection against arbitrary harm depend entirely on a final classification decision.

This principle does not eliminate evidence or adjudication. It determines what should happen while evidence and adjudication remain incomplete.

## 6. A proposed formal model of CyberLaw fairness

The following model is an extension for discussion. It is not already present in the article.

### 6.1 Three layers

A decision should pass through three layers in order.

#### Layer A: Baseline safeguards

Ask whether the process preserves:

- Consideration of affected interests.
- Accessible participation or appropriate representation.
- Relevant privacy.
- Freedom from irrelevant exclusion.
- Understandable reasons.
- Independent challenge.
- Protection against arbitrary harm.

#### Layer B: Justification for restrictions

For a proposed restriction \(a\), define:

\[
J(a)
=
P(a)\land E(a)\land N(a)\land L(a)\land Q(a)\land V(a),
\]

where:

- \(P\): a legitimate protective purpose is stated.
- \(E\): the evidence is adequate for this action.
- \(N\): the action is necessary.
- \(L\): no less harmful feasible alternative provides adequate protection.
- \(Q\): burdens are proportionate.
- \(V\): review and remedy are available.

Under this proposed rule, failure of a required condition blocks ordinary authorization. Any emergency exception must itself be separately defined, limited, and reviewable.

#### Layer C: Choice among permissible alternatives

Only after baseline safeguards and justification checks should the institution compare permissible alternatives.

This avoids a dangerous design in which a sufficiently high benefit score simply cancels privacy, standing, or appeal.

### 6.2 Role-reversal test

For parties \(x\) and \(y\):

> Would the decision rule remain acceptable if their positions of power, familiarity, and control were exchanged while the relevant interests and evidence remained the same?

This does not prove fairness, but it can expose rules that quietly privilege the familiar or powerful.

### 6.3 Irreversibility test

A general proposed decision principle is:

\[
\text{Required justification increases with intrusiveness and irreversibility.}
\]

That is an ordering principle, not a calibrated mathematical law.

A temporary accommodation and an irreversible destructive intervention should not require the same kind or strength of justification.

### 6.4 Outcome versus procedure

Define:

\[
F_{\text{process}}(d)
=
A(d)\land P(d)\land E(d)\land R(d)\land M(d),
\]

where:

- \(A\): access and representation.
- \(P\): privacy and non-coercion.
- \(E\): relevant evidence and explained reasoning.
- \(R\): review and appeal.
- \(M\): remedy and correction.

Passing these checks establishes procedural compliance with this proposed specification. It does not prove perfect substantive justice.

A fair-looking procedure can still omit an affected interest. That possibility is why review must remain open.

## 7. What machine verification can and cannot establish

The article calls the framework machine-verifiable, but does not yet provide a formal language, executable rules, or a verification method. [51]

### Potentially verifiable properties

Given an explicit specification and trustworthy inputs, software could check whether:

- An accommodation request received a recorded response.
- A decision contains required reasons.
- A representative’s conflict declaration was completed.
- An appeal route was supplied.
- A review deadline passed.
- A required authorization was present.
- A prohibited disclosure field was absent.
- A remedy was assigned and followed up.

### Properties not established by those checks alone

The same software would not thereby establish:

- Whether an unfamiliar entity is conscious.
- Whether testimony is truthful.
- Whether all affected interests were identified.
- Whether a representative genuinely understands the represented party.
- Whether a legal conclusion is binding.
- Whether an outcome is absolutely fair.

The technically defensible claim is:

> CyberLaw can make specified procedural duties machine-checkable while leaving contested facts, interpretation, and authority subject to accountable review.

That is a substantial engineering goal without overstating what verification means.

## 8. The balance between CyberLaw and existing law

### 8.1 Moral consideration, legal personality, and standing are different

- Moral consideration asks whether interests should count.
- Legal personality concerns recognition within a legal system.
- Standing concerns access to a particular adjudicative process.
- Legal capacity concerns the ability to hold or exercise legal rights and duties.
- Representation concerns who acts for whom.
- Enforceability concerns who can compel compliance.

CyberLaw connects these concepts, but a complete implementation must specify each separately.

### 8.2 Te Awa Tupua supports representation, not a universal conclusion

The New Zealand statute declares Te Awa Tupua a legal person and provides that its rights, powers, and duties are exercised through Te Pou Tupua. [54]

This supports the article’s argument that legal systems can construct representation for nonhuman interests.

It does not establish that every possible entity already has equivalent legal personality.

The example should also retain its specific statutory and Whanganui settlement context rather than being reduced to a generic technological analogy.

### 8.3 Animal sentience law supports differentiated recognition

The UK legislation includes nonhuman vertebrates, cephalopod molluscs, and decapod crustaceans within its definition of animal. [68]

This supports the proposition that law can identify and protect nonhuman interests.

It does not create universal personhood or identical rights for all lifeforms.

### 8.4 The robotics resolution is a debate precedent

The European Parliament’s 2017 resolution included a recommendation to consider, in the long run, an electronic-person status connected to liability for sophisticated autonomous robots. [59][66]

It should not be described as having established general AI personhood.

Its relevance is narrower: legal institutions have considered whether existing categories adequately allocate responsibility for autonomous systems.

### 8.5 Accessibility provides an established bridge

The CRPD addresses equal recognition, support in exercising legal capacity, and reasonable accommodation. [53]

This is a strong source for the article’s access and participation mechanics, while remaining distinct from the draft’s broader proposed coverage.

### 8.6 A private governance framework does not automatically create public authority

CyberCongress needs an explicit jurisdictional basis.

Possible mechanisms for further development include:

- Voluntarily adopted organizational rules.
- Contractual commitments where legally valid.
- Independent advisory review.
- Ombuds and grievance procedures.
- Policy proposals.
- Legislation.

Each requires its own authority, scope, and limits.

The framework can establish demanding internal duties without representing itself as overriding courts, public law, or the rights of nonparticipants.

## 9. Strong challenges—and constructive responses

| Challenge | Why it matters | Strongest constructive response |
|---|---|---|
| “Anything could claim rights.” | Unlimited claims could overwhelm institutions. | Separate respectful intake and basic precautions from evidence-intensive remedies and legal recognition. |
| “The framework equates every form of existence.” | Different interests may require incompatible protections. | Equal consideration does not mean identical interests, rights, or allocations. |
| “Tiering recreates hierarchy.” | Classification could become a worth ranking. | Preserve baseline safeguards and use overlapping interest profiles to assign duties. |
| “Unknown interests cannot be represented.” | Proxies can project their own preferences. | Use bounded mandates, uncertainty records, independent review, and replacement procedures. |
| “Advanced systems can imitate preference.” | Expression alone may be misleading. | Separate expression, corroboration, provisional protection, and classification; do not rely on one universal test. |
| “Absolute fairness is impossible.” | Knowledge, resources, and institutions are limited. | Make the duty unconditional while making findings revisable and remedies obligatory. |
| “An immutable ledger guarantees justice.” | Record integrity is not truth or legitimacy. | Treat audit integrity, evidence quality, privacy, and institutional independence as separate requirements. |
| “Coexistence rules ignore emergencies.” | Immediate threats may require action. | Add narrowly bounded emergency procedures with least-harm duties and prompt independent review. |
| “The system can be captured by replicas.” | Replication could distort representation or resource claims. | Do not equate copy count with voting weight or unlimited resource entitlement; govern mandates and conflicts explicitly. |
| “Existing law does not recognize CyberLaw.” | Normative authority is not enforceable jurisdiction. | Specify whether each provision is ethical guidance, an internal rule, a contractual commitment, or a legislative proposal. |

These responses strengthen the framework by answering implementation questions without abandoning its central non-domination principle.

## 10. Illustrative stress tests

The following cases are hypothetical tests, not reports of actual events.

### Case A: Communication access without intimate disclosure

An augmented participant requests text communication, additional response time, and a private correction channel.

A CyberLaw-consistent response would assess and provide the access pathway without demanding a neural inventory or proof of a private companion system.

The fairness question is whether participation is effective—not whether the institution can classify every aspect of the person.

### Case B: An unfamiliar laboratory specimen

A specimen’s responses do not match familiar behavioral tests.

Negative-inference protection prevents treating that mismatch alone as proof of no interests. Appropriate precautions and independent review can be considered while the relevant evidence remains uncertain.

This does not require a predetermined conclusion about sentience.

### Case C: A reported artificial existence facing shutdown

A system produces statements expressing a preference to continue.

The decision procedure should separate:

- What the statements establish.
- What remains uncertain.
- Immediate safety needs.
- Confidential preservation options, if lawful and appropriate.
- Ownership and operational authority.
- Independent review.

Neither automatic recognition nor automatic dismissal follows solely from the statement.

### Case D: Habitat interests and public health

A proposed habitat protection conflicts with a substantiated public-health concern.

A fair analysis includes affected people, organisms, ecological relationships, feasible alternatives, the consequences of delay, and the distribution of burdens.

It does not resolve the case by declaring one category categorically worthless.

### Case E: A wrongful permanent record

A classification is later overturned, but the original entry contains sensitive personal information.

A supersession notice may correct the procedural history without removing the continuing disclosure harm.

The response may therefore require both an audit correction and deletion or restriction of confidential content under the applicable retention rules.

### Case F: CyberCongress deadlock

Interest constituencies cannot agree while preventable harm continues.

A complete constitution needs a temporary protective pathway, a time limit, independent review, and a prohibition on using deadlock as indefinite authorization for the status quo.

## 11. Proposed fairness clauses for further drafting

The following clauses are proposed additions, not statements already present in version 1.0.

### Baseline consideration

No affected party shall be excluded from consideration solely because of origin, appearance, substrate, unfamiliarity, communication difference, or inability to participate without support.

### Classification restraint

Unresolved classification shall not, by itself, authorize destruction, coercive disclosure, confinement, or denial of an otherwise available accommodation.

### Specificity of protection

Protections shall respond to affected interests and foreseeable harms rather than functioning as a general ranking of worth.

### Supported participation

Direct participation shall be supported where possible. Representation shall be limited, accountable, reviewable, and replaceable.

### Privacy and non-interference

Respectful acknowledgment shall not require technical certification or disclosure of private neural, physiological, medical, biometric, companion-system, or operational information.

### Restriction justification

Any restriction shall identify its purpose, evidence, necessity, less harmful alternatives, duration, responsible authority, and review mechanism.

### Emergency limits

Emergency measures shall be limited to the protective need, documented when safely possible, and reviewed promptly. Emergency authority shall not become indefinite authority.

### Audit and retention

Procedural accountability shall not require permanent publication or retention of sensitive case content.

### Remedies

A finding of wrong shall address cessation, restoration where possible, correction, recurrence prevention, and accessible follow-up.

### Authority

Every decision shall identify whether its authority is advisory, organizational, contractual, or statutory, and shall not imply powers it does not possess.

## 15. Final interpretation

CyberLaw’s distinctive ambition is not simply to expand the list of protected entities. It is to prevent uncertainty, unfamiliarity, ownership, or institutional power from becoming automatic permission to disregard an affected existence. Its strongest mechanics are therefore the combination of non-contingent consideration, accessible expression, privacy, representation, justified restrictions, and reversible institutional decisions. [51]

The next stage is to make those mechanics explicit enough that a decision-maker can be challenged on a specific failure:

- Which affected interest was omitted?
- Which communication barrier was left in place?
- Which disclosure was unnecessary?
- Which restriction lacked justification?
- Which representative lacked authority?
- Which less harmful alternative was ignored?
- Which appeal was inaccessible?
- Which remedy remains incomplete?

Under that interpretation, absolute fairness is not absolute certainty. It is the refusal to let uncertainty cancel accountable duties.
