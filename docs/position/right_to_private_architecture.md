# The Right to Private Architecture

## Position

Every person should be able to maintain a private, non-commercial, body-
integrated, assistive, communication, continuity, or personal technical
architecture without being forced to make that architecture public.

Private architecture may include a person's own vocabulary for body–mind
integration, assistive communication, continuity needs, companion context,
screenless interaction, privacy boundaries, or other voluntary self-description.

The existence of a privacy boundary is not evidence that the underlying private
experience, preference, system, or communication need is false. Public
non-documentation is a privacy feature.

## Privacy, bodily integrity, and autonomy

The right to private architecture is grounded in familiar principles:

- Privacy protects people from arbitrary or unnecessary intrusion into personal
  information and communication.
- Bodily integrity supports a person's authority over information and decisions
  involving their own body and body-integrated context.
- Autonomy supports the ability to describe oneself, set communication
  boundaries, and seek accessible interaction without a compulsory performance
  of proof.
- Equal access requires that a person can request information, correction,
  assistance, cooling, rest, shade, accessible communication, or review without
  being excluded because their terminology is private or nonstandard.
- Non-interference requires that documentation, analysis, research, and
  governance tools do not become instruments for forced disclosure,
  surveillance, credentialing, manipulation, or control.

These principles apply whether a person uses conventional, emerging, personal,
assistive, or private terminology. Respectful treatment does not depend on a
public registry, a technical inventory, a diagnosis, a certification, a device
identifier, or institutional validation.

## Public non-documentation

The absence of public documentation can mean many things:

- A person has chosen privacy.
- The information is not relevant to the public task.
- The information is held only in a personal, protected, or offline context.
- Disclosure could create surveillance, discrimination, extraction, security,
  accessibility, or dignity harms.
- The information has not been formalized as a public category and does not need
  to be formalized to support respectful treatment.

Therefore:

> Public non-documentation is not a reliable basis for denying a person's stated
> communication boundary, access request, correction request, or need for
> respectful handling.

A platform or institution may distinguish between a private self-description
and a factual claim requiring evidence for a particular technical, clinical,
legal, or safety process. But it must make that distinction narrowly, identify
the exact necessary input, explain the purpose, use the minimum necessary
information, provide an accessible fallback, and preserve correction and appeal.

## Practical duties

Organizations handling private architecture should:

- Preserve user-defined terminology in the scope of the requested task.
- Avoid identity, health, disability, augmentation, capability, or credibility
  inference from language, behavior, communication mode, or nondisclosure.
- Request only task-specific, minimum-necessary information.
- Separate content-bearing information from minimal audit records.
- Apply purpose limitation, retention limits, deletion, correction, and appeal.
- Provide plain-language explanation, keyboard and screen-reader-compatible
  interaction, human fallback, no-time-pressure completion, and offline or
  save-return options where relevant.
- Keep technical outputs advisory unless a separately governed and reviewed
  process requires another output type.
- Prohibit the conversion of private terms into a public credential, ranking,
  service-access criterion, enforcement label, or resource-allocation variable.

## Conclusion

The right to private architecture is not a demand for public certification. It
is a demand for privacy, autonomy, respectful communication, accessibility, and
equal service access.

A person should not have to surrender private terminology, body-integrated
context, communication preferences, or continuity boundaries in order to be
treated with dignity, receive accessible information, request correction, or
participate in civic, research, platform, health, or institutional processes.
