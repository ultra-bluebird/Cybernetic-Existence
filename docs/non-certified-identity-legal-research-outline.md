# Non-Certified Identity Recognition in Smart City Governance

## Scope and Research Position

This outline identifies legal and policy principles that can support a municipal or smart-city system in respecting self-declared, non-certified identity and service preferences without invasive proof.

It does not assert that “cybernetic host” is already an independently protected legal class in every jurisdiction. It does not provide legal advice or predict litigation outcomes. It provides a source-oriented research structure for Augmented-Hub and participating institutions to use when drafting voluntary policy, conducting impact assessments, and obtaining jurisdiction-specific legal review.

The strongest practical argument is usually not that a city must certify a new identity category. It is that a city can and should avoid unnecessary proof demands when providing dignity, accessibility, privacy, communication preference, human review, and ordinary public service access.

## International Human Rights Foundation

### Human Dignity and Equal Respect

Use the following proposition:

> Public systems should respect a person’s self-description and protect equal civic participation unless a narrowly defined, lawful, necessary, and proportionate purpose requires verification of a specific fact.

Supporting research directions:

- Universal human-rights principles of dignity, equality, privacy, expression, participation, and non-discrimination.
- International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protections relevant to privacy, equality, and recognition before the law.
- International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights protections relevant to health, education, culture, and adequate living conditions.
- Regional human-rights instruments where a participating city operates.

Augmented-Hub policy use:

- Treat self-declared cybernetic-host status as a voluntary dignity and service-preference field.
- Do not convert self-description into compulsory medical, biometric, neural, or device proof.
- Provide an accessible correction, complaint, and human-review path.

### Disability Rights and Accessibility

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities establishes equality and non-discrimination principles and treats accessibility as essential to independent living and full participation. Article 9 addresses access to the physical environment, transportation, information, communications, and public services. [229][226]

Augmented-Hub policy use:

- Provide accessibility and accommodation through functional access needs, not invasive diagnosis demands.
- Offer offline, in-person, and human-assisted public-service paths.
- Support assistive and augmentative technology use without treating it as a suspicious or adverse characteristic.
- Make grievance, appeal, public-information, and civic-participation systems accessible.

### Privacy and Data Protection

Data-protection principles support the separation of service access from unnecessary identity collection.

Relevant research topics:

- Lawful basis and purpose limitation.
- Data minimization.
- Storage limitation.
- Security and confidentiality.
- Data-subject access, correction, and deletion rights.
- Transparency around automated processing.
- Limits on sensitive-data processing.
- Privacy impact assessment requirements.

Augmented-Hub policy use:

- Collect only the smallest self-attested preference needed for the service.
- Do not collect augmentation details, implant information, neural data, biometrics, clinical records, or telemetry for ordinary recognition or accessibility.
- Make declaration voluntary, revocable, exportable, and deletable where applicable.
- Do not use self-declared status for enforcement, surveillance, eligibility restriction, or adverse profiling.

## GDPR and Automated Decisions

GDPR Article 22 states a right not to be subject to a solely automated decision, including profiling, that produces legal effects or similarly significant effects, subject to defined exceptions. [224][227]

For Augmented-Hub policy design, this supports:

- Avoiding solely automated final decisions in high-impact municipal services.
- Providing meaningful human review, explanation, correction, and contestability.
- Documenting the purpose, model logic, data categories, and appeal mechanism.
- Avoiding use of self-attested cybernetic status as an adverse model feature.
- Using data minimization and purpose limitation for any preference record.

The framework’s jurisdictional applicability depends on whether the system processes personal data subject to GDPR or comparable law. It should be reviewed in the relevant legal jurisdiction.

## EU AI Act Research Direction

The EU AI Act’s Article 27 framework requires certain public bodies and public-service deployers of high-risk AI systems to conduct a fundamental-rights impact assessment before deployment, covering affected groups, specific risks, human oversight, and mitigation measures. [219][220]

Augmented-Hub policy use:

- Conduct a rights-impact assessment before a consequential municipal automation is used.
- Document affected service groups without creating an identity registry.
- Identify foreseeable accessibility, privacy, discrimination, and service-denial risks.
- Record human-oversight measures, complaint mechanisms, and mitigation actions.
- Publish an accessible public summary where appropriate.

## Structured Argument for Policy Defense

### Claim One: Respect Is Not Certification

A city can respect voluntary terminology, communication preferences, privacy choices, and accessibility requests without making a legal, medical, or technical certification.

Evidence structure:

- The request is voluntary.
- No essential service requires the declaration.
- The city records only the minimum service preference.
- The city does not validate or retain intimate technical details.
- The city provides equal baseline service to people who decline to declare.

### Claim Two: Privacy Minimization Is More Proportionate

Requiring invasive proof to provide ordinary respect or access creates privacy and dignity risks without a demonstrated need.

Evidence structure:

- Identify the municipal purpose.
- Show that a lower-data alternative achieves the purpose.
- Document retention limits and deletion controls.
- Show that sensitive augmentation, neural, biometric, and health data are not necessary.
- Provide a human alternative where automation cannot accommodate the preference.

### Claim Three: Accessibility Is a Public-Service Design Duty

A service should be usable by people with varied communication, mobility, sensory, cognitive, and assistive-technology needs without forcing disclosure of why a specific format or route is useful.

Evidence structure:

- Provide accessible formats and offline paths by default.
- Use functional service options rather than diagnostic labels.
- Document accessibility testing and community review.
- Maintain accessible complaint and remedy processes.

### Claim Four: Automated Systems Require Accountability

A city should not use self-declared identity or proxy features as adverse automation inputs, and it should provide review and appeal for consequential outcomes.

Evidence structure:

- Feature inventory and prohibited-use list.
- Disparate-impact and accessibility audit.
- Human review path.
- Explanation and correction process.
- Periodic independent audit and public reporting.

## Source Plan

For each jurisdiction, retain:

- Official treaty or constitutional text.
- Official statutes and regulations.
- Official court opinions or authoritative case databases.
- Official municipal code and administrative rules.
- Data-protection authority guidance.
- Disability-rights authority guidance.
- Procurement and algorithmic-governance policy.
- Privacy impact assessment and civil-rights impact assessment records.
- Community consultation record.

Avoid relying solely on commentary or vendor claims. Distinguish clearly among binding law, administrative guidance, institutional policy, technical standard, and policy proposal.
